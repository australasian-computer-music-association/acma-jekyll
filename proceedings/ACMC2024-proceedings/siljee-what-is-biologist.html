What is a biologist doing at a computer music conference? – Sonification of mass spectrometry data

Sam Siljee

In ACMC 2024 Proceedings, The Australian Institute of Music, Sydney and Melbourne, Australia, pp. 65–67. Australasian Computer Music Association.

Abstract

Background

Mass spectrometry (MS) is a technique used in biology to analyse complex mixtures. In my work studying lung cancer I use this method to survey thousands of different proteins in human cells. MS works with electromagnetic fields to quantify and identify ionised samples. A mass spectrum is a set of thousands of peaks with an intensity and mass to change ratio (m/z) value. The dataset used for this work contains 58,248 spectra, observed over a 90 minute experiment. There are two types of spectra; overview spectra for quantification, paired with twenty fragmentation spectra for identification. MS data can no longer be manually analysed because of the magnitude of modern experiments. Conventional analysis involves layers of statistical summarisation followed by visualisation. In this work I challenge the idea that MS data can only be presented visually.

Methods

Previous sonifications of scientific data often involve mapping a parameter to the pitch of a synthesiser. This arbitrarily constrains scale, tone, and quantisation. This project seeks to honour the underlying raw data as the source of sound. Complex waveforms can be constructed by the addition of simpler waveforms according to the Fourier Theory. Unique tones were made for each spectrum by adding a sine wave for every peak with m/z for frequency and intensity for amplitude. These 58,248 tones were then compiled into a soundscape using the time point of observation and overall intensity of the spectrum. The two types of spectra naturally form a two-channel audio format.

Results

The tones have a ringing quality, emerging from many interference patterns. The rhythm reflects the acquisition cycle of the mass spectrometer. The overview spectra can be heard to gradually change over time as proteins enter the mass spectrometer, whereas the identification spectra are chaotic with proteins rapidly fragmented for identification. Artefact, background noise, and contaminants are also audible. While this methodology is unlikely to replace conventional analysis methods for gaining biological understanding, it can be used as a tool for quality control for MS data, or as an aid in teaching MS. Another exciting implication is making complex MS experiments more accessible. Not only are there scientists with visual impairment, but audio can be less intimidating for a lay audience. This project has opened up interesting conversations with friends and family around complexity, large datasets, dynamic range, signal/noise, and how different cancer really is from normal cells.

Conclusion

This project presents a method to generate soundscapes from raw mass spectrometry data. This project honours the original data by using it as the fundamental source of the tones. It presents an interesting methodology to summarise a large dataset into human interpretable form and opens up discussions on MS and current challenges in science.
