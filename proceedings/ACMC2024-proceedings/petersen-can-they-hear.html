Can they hear the difference? Structured improvisation versus common-practice compositional methods in Guitar Science application

Kris Petersen

In ACMC 2024 Proceedings, The Australian Institute of Music, Sydney and Melbourne, Australia, pp. 79–80. Australasian Computer Music Association.

Abstract

Guitar Science (GS) is a studio methodological process created to emulate the phenomenological ambience associated with the live-concert experience. The emulation is achieved through compositional and production processes primarily focused on layering background guitar parts and mixing within the three-dimensional mix-box to perceive Sound Pressure Levels (SPLs) in a large-scale environment. However, the fundamental flaw in the research to date is that GS application is assumed to have equal weighting in both compositional and production processes and, as such, requires an increased amount of time and financial resources in the studio. As a result, there is a limitation in current practitioners to those who are aware of the process and have a disregard for the costs in their creative process. Therefore, this research aims to discover if a ratio of composition and production exists in the researcher’s autoethnographic process that potentially becomes a springboard for other GS practitioners and researchers. Three songs, with the GS parts using alternate compositional processes, were recorded. The (1) first was classified as common-practice techniques (CPT) involving Baroque compositional techniques and the researcher’s pre-existing knowledge of orchestration, harmony, and voice leading. The (2) second was structure improvisation (SI), the technique established in the broader research arch for GS composition. Both compositions were applied to the same song and performed with a string quartet. With a substantial differentiation of time associated with the two techniques, CPT focused on compositional considerations while SI was production focused. The recordings acted as comparative media in a reception analysis study. Forty-five participants were recruited and entered the three-phase designed study. Phase one was a generalised demographic questionnaire to contextualise the participants and their interview responses. During the phase two interviews, the participants listened to the CPT and SI versions of the three songs. Their preference, the reasoning behind their selection, and their perception of emotional content were the process’s focus. Within phase three, the participants were shown a multimedia presentation and interviewed on their perception of GS application. The quantitatively collated data was analysed, and subsequent conclusions were explored, considering the phase one questionnaire’s phenomenological underpinning. The results demonstrated that within the sample group, the audible showed a pre-disposition to SI within the research media. Furthermore, the data demonstrated that GS application within the third phase was generally recognised. The research demonstrates that the scrutiny traditionally associated with the compositional process may not be necessary for GS application and that the process may dominate as a production in nature. While the study has autoethnographic and phenomenological underpinnings due to the compositional process and third-party participation, the findings for other GS practitioners and researchers could provide a precedent for limiting the scope of their practice while opening the discussion for alternate GS applications.
