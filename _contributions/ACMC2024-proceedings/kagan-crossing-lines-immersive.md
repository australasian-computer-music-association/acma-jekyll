Video games are one of the largest and most profitable entertainment industries in the world, with a global market value far exceeding the film and music industries combined (Divers, 2023). It was only shortly after the wider acceptance of computers that sound and audio started to play a fundamental immersive, affective, and contextual role in computer programming and design (Collins, 2008, p. 28). The study of video game music, Ludomusicology, is a growing field that is exploring the ways in which games have expanded musical expression and continue to push the boundaries of how music and gaming can intersect. One of the most fundamental aspects that set games apart from other sources of media is their non-linear interactive elements (Collins, 2008, p. 142), yet most games utilise only very basic interactive audio systems. Interaction is frequently only present in small ways, with simple transitions or states controlling the music, however deeper levels of musical interactivity can promote stronger levels of immersion (Kagan, 2020). Some games take elements of interactivity further and connect gameplay and music on a fundamental design level. These mechanisms often are approached as both overt musical connections, such as beat synchronisation locked to core gameplay elements like shooting and reloading in BPM: Bullets Per Minute (Awe Interactive, 2022), or covert connections where the audio functions either in a supporting or hidden fashion (Kagan, 2023; Stevens et al., 2015). By examining the ways these overt and covert synchronisations can foster increased levels of immersion, we can delve further into understanding some potential design principals and how they can be explored to push the medium of music/gameplay synchronicity even further.
