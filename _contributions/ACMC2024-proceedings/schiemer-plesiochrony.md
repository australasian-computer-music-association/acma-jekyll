This year marks a musical centenary that laid a foundation for the work you are about to hear, Plesiochrony. George Antheil's Ballet Mécanique, which premiered in Paris in 1924, featured an array of bespoke musical instruments designed and built by the composer. Among these was a pneumatic mechanism for synchronising multiple player pianos that became the unlikely inspiration for a patent awarded in 1942 for a secret communications device. The Lamarr-Antheil patent is generally acknowledged by engineers as pivotal to the development of frequency hopping, a foundation of mobile phone communication. The inspiration for it came in Hollywood where Antheil met his collaborator, Hedy Kiesler Markey, an Austrian actress, better known as Hedy Lamarr. She migrated to the US after leaving her former husband, Fritz Mandl, a controversial businessman who had worked for the Nazis after the forced acquisition of his munitions factory following the anschluss. The Lamarr-Antheil patent specified an anti-jamming device for radio controlled torpedoes and was gifted to the US military as part of the allied war effort. It was first used during the Cuban missiles crisis and when the patent expired in the 1980s, became crucial in global engineering initiatives to make better use of the broadcast spectrum and paving the way for mobile phone communication. It seems fitting that a work for mobile phones commemorates the centenary of a work that changed the way we communicate. Plesiochrony uses multiple microtonal scales devised by contemporary tuning theorist and instrument builder, Erv Wilson using a method called Combination Product Sets (CPS). Dekanies are microtonal 10-note CPS scales created by multiplying all pairs of a set of 5 harmonic factors. Multiple dekanies explore the nexus between, melody, rhythm and timbre as they flow seamlessly from one scale to another in a shared listening space. The concert space becomes a giant resonator excited by a malleable array of hand-held fold-back speakers and simultaneously, a shared listening space where an audience can experience electronic music in exactly the same way concert audiences experience music collectively, i.e. without artificial reverb, mains-powered amplification or head-phones.
