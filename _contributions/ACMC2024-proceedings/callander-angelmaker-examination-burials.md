British electronic musician, Burial, is best known for Untrue (2007), the Mercury Music Prize-nominated album assembled using unorthodox audio editing software and source materials. This paper examines one of Burial’s production approaches: the use and reuse of vocal samples across multiple tracks. We used ‘unmixing’ software to separate vocal ‘stems’ to then chart and analyse three sampled sources and their reuse across a body of work spanning seventeen years. Drawing on a theoretical approach from Gilles Deleuze and Felix Guattari and an abstracted idea of repetition from Adam Harper, we argue that, in an era of unlimited access to larger suites of digital audio, the redeployment of identifiable vocal samples is unique to Burial amongst modern electronic dance music producers. This paper is pertinent to discourse on sampling and music production as it identifies a self-referential approach that transcends single arrangements or albums and presents ideas and qualities shared across a decades-long body of work. It is significant to broader discourse on the digital age for identifying a practice that can be read as a self-imposed limitation in defiance of abundant resources. It also represents a distinct methodology that focuses on materiality and relationality in the analysis of electronic music.
