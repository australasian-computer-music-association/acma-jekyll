Renowned for its signal-processing potency, granular synthesis treats input sounds as masses of audio slices referred to as 'grains’. These grains can be sculpted into a myriad of complex textures and form a wide range of sound objects with different qualities. If we look beyond its original quantum physical metaphors in Dennis Gabor's particle-based sound analysis in the mid-20th century, however, it is possible to draw a number of metaphorical parallels with physical observations from the much more ancient Greco-Roman philosophy of Epicureanism. As an atomist philosophy, the Epicurean model of reality derives everything in our universe from two simple elements: 'atoms', the smallest unbreakable units of matter in constant motion, and 'void', the space through which atoms move. This short paper presents the author’s on-going practice-based research project that investigates the connections between granular synthesis and Epicurean physics, manifested through interpretive soundscape composition. The investigation is motivated on a desire to contribute a useful methodology for inspiration in sonic art practice, showing how technology, composition, and philosophy can be used to form a cohesive creative vision. It also extends his personal artistic interests in exploring the sublimity and latent beauty of nature, inspired by the didactic Epicurean poetry in 'De Rerum Natura/The Nature of Things' (Lucretius, c. 99-55 BC). An analysis of the project's first formal composition, 'Matter and Void', elucidates the author’s interdisciplinary methodology. This work highlights several key Epicurean concepts – the atom, void, emergence, and randomness/agency through atomic 'swerve' – applied to sound generation with several custom granular synthesis patches under development in Max/MSP. Audio examples from the composing process reveal musical translations: sound grain shape and waveform (atom); manipulations of grains through spectral, temporal, and dynamic/image space (void); timbral, melodic, and structural development through re-granulation, extra effects processing and acoustic recordings (emergence); and granular stochasticity and sound object interactions (swerve). To conclude, the influence of scientific phenomena on the author’s practice in forming a dialogue with the paper-and-ink medium of Lucretius' Epicurean poetry is discussed, detailing the retrospectively futuristic goal of the project: renewing ancient ideas through technologically mediated creativity.
