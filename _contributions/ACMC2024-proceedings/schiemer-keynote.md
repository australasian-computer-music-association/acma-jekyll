This year marks 50 years since Australian musical Instrument designer Tony Furse built the Qasar Multimode 8, aka the Qasar M8, or simply the M8, one of the first digital music synthesisers ever built and quite possibly the first entirely digital design. This keynote will summarise the historical significance of innovations such as the M8 digital audio synthesis engine, its QDOS operating system for sharing memory between dual processors, its GUI, and more. The M8 has provided the technological pathway that led to the development of the more widely known and commercially successful Fairlight CMI but also paved the way for developments in computing more generally. What began as a project to recover and digitise files by Banks’ successors at the Sydney Conservatorium and the Canberra School of Music has gradually shed light on the M8’s legacy and challenged a popular narrative about Australia’s role in the development of computer music. The keynote examines the role of Furse’s principal electronic music collaborators from the Canberra School of Music played during the development of the M8. These include composers Don Banks who recognised the M8’s transformative potential and John Crocker, the technician responsible for programming the first music composition software for the M8. MUSEQ8, the music sequence compiler, and PLAYIT, the software interface for playing composed sequences appears to have influenced the development of Fairlight’s Music Composition Language (MCL) and Real Time Composer. In an effort to trace the chronological transition from the Qasar M8 to the Fairlight CMI the keynote will focus on the recent effort made in collaboration with Furse to recover software from original 8-inch floppy disks nearing the end their expected service life. I will also discuss some of the novel recovery techniques we developed to salvage disk images from ageing media, extract files from deprecated operating systems, save them in formats independent of the original hardware architecture and share the original work on-line using real-time audio synthesis running in web browsers. This historic recovery effort is both a work in progress and a race against time.
