The many-worlds theory of H. Everett is an interpretation of quantum physics. According to this theory, every time a random event could have happened, the universe splits into two: in one copy of the universe the random event happened; in the other it did not. Thus there are an alternative number of “alternative universes”, with new ones splitting off all the time. Everett advanced this theory as a replacement for the probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics, which is what Einstein was objecting to when he said “God does not play dice”. In this piece the alternative universes are represented by two slowly diverging pitches and by randomly arranged snippets from computer games, the alternative universes most accessible to us.

Many Worlds Theory has as framework part of a work for solo harpsichord by J.S. Bach (the Adagio from BWV 974). This work is itself a transcription from the different world of an oboe concerto by the Venetian composer Alessandro Marcello (though it was earlier thought to be a transcription of a Vivaldi violin concerto). The original oboe melody is represented in the tape part in greatly slowed-down form (like the Chinese melodies in Japanese Tōgaku court music); much of the harpsichord part consists of computer-generated elaborations of parts of Bach’s elaboration of the oboe melody.

Many Worlds Theory is dedicated to the harpsichordist extraordinaire Winsome Evans, who offered much help and encouragement during the composition of the piece, and who premiered the work on October 13, 2001 at the Old Darlington School, University of Sydney.
