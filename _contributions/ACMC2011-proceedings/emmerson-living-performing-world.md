There has been a steady erosion of the ‘living/non-living’ dichotomy (of which ‘organic/inorganic’ is a version). This has given way to a more ecological model in which ‘living’ becomes an activity rather than a state [Ingold 6]. Until a century or so ago the activity was limited to the cause/effect of human body action, but the sounding effect may now be displaced entirely from the original cause, both in time and space. This is ‘action over a distance’ rather than ‘dislocation’ – and technology might become the healer. Due to recording, the sounding world itself has become an ever wider source of musical material. More recently it has made a transition to becoming a fully fledged ‘musical instrument’. I will suggest that in this mediatised world ‘liveness’ is a network of activity in which subjects (performers) and objects (performed), causes and effects, can never unambiguously be ‘sorted out’ (our musical equivalent of the Turing test may not be very conclusive). Ingold’s point is that in many societies apparently inorganic (‘non-living’) objects are imbued with a spirit – an animistic world – which may be related to in the same way as to fellow creatures. Clearly the Schaefferian world of reduced listening has here transformed. How do we feel when we activate this world as live music? All kinds of causes may be evoked - breath, friction, percussion are inherited from the mechanical world, to which bio-activity and perhaps imagination itself may be harnessed to play our ‘animate net-work’.
